Most SETI research asks: _are they out there?_ Clément Vidal and twenty co-authors ask a different question: _if they were out there, what would we actually look for?_[^paper] Their 118-page review, originating from a 2023 workshop at the Penn State SETI Symposium, is less a survey of results than a **catalog of possibilities** — a systematic enumeration of every proposed technosignature, organized by spatial scale from the ground beneath our feet to the observable universe itself.

[^paper]: Clément Vidal, Benji L. Fields, Damian R. Sowinski, Mark Elowitz, Stuart Bartlett, Richard J. Terrile, Alex Ellery, Daliah Bibas, Armando M. Mastrogiovanni, Niklas Döbler, Manika Singla, Julia DeMarines, Theresa Fisher, Yuri Uno, Jake D. Turner, Evan L. Sneed, Advait Huggahalli, Megan Grace Li, Chester Li, Macy Huston, and Ramiro Saide, "The Search for Technosignatures: a Review of Possibilities," arXiv:2605.21093 (May 2026).

The result is something unusual in the literature: not a status report on existing searches, but a **design space** for future ones. And its organizing principle — scale — reveals a surprising amount about how we think about extraterrestrial intelligence, and where our assumptions quietly constrain us.

## Start at Your Feet

The review opens not with radio telescopes pointed at distant stars, but with Earth. The logic is disarming: if a technological civilization existed anywhere in the solar system during the past four billion years, what traces might survive?

The concept is the **shadow technosphere** — an extension of the "shadow biosphere" idea to technological artifacts. The proposed signatures are startlingly concrete. Mining operations would leave anomalous mineral depletion patterns. Industrial activity would deposit persistent organic pollutants (like PFAS) in sedimentary layers. Fossil fuel combustion alters $${}^{12}\text{C}/{}^{13}\text{C}$$ isotope ratios. Nuclear technology leaves unmistakable fingerprints: Curium-247, Plutonium-244, Iodine-129 — isotopes with no plausible natural production pathway at the concentrations that would signal industrial activity.

The authors even point to a real example of how this search might work. In 1972, the Oklo natural uranium reactor was discovered in Gabon — a geological formation where sustained nuclear fission occurred naturally two billion years ago. Initial suspicion that it was artificial turned out to be wrong, but the investigative framework is exactly what a technosignature search requires: identify an anomaly, enumerate hypotheses, test them.

Then there is **genomic SETI** — the proposal that messages could be encoded in non-coding DNA, whether as evidence of directed panspermia or deliberate communication. The idea sounds like science fiction until you remember that DNA is an extraordinarily stable information storage medium, that non-coding regions are under weaker selective pressure, and that we now have the sequencing technology to search systematically for anomalous information content.

## The Moon, the Lagrange Points, and the Art of Hiding in Plain Sight

Moving outward, the review catalogs solar system locations where artifacts might persist. The Moon receives extended treatment for good reason: no atmosphere means no erosion, no geological recycling, and minimal meteoroid flux on the farside. The Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter already images the surface at 0.5-meter resolution — enough to clearly resolve Apollo landing sites, descent modules, EVA tracks, and equipment packages.

The authors describe the **Paracelsus C structures**: unusual linear and rectangular features on a farside crater floor, visible at 0.5m resolution, with three-dimensional relief confirmed by cast shadows. Natural explanations exist (impact erosion, tectonic contraction, melt pools), but the features remain formally unexplained. The point is not that these are artificial — almost certainly they are not — but that the resolution and coverage now exist to conduct this search systematically rather than anecdotally.

The Earth-Moon and Earth-Sun **Lagrange points** are treated as natural monitoring stations. L4 and L5 are gravitationally stable; an object placed there billions of years ago could remain today with no propellant. The authors note a particularly elegant hiding place: **horseshoe orbits**, where an object spends centuries cycling between L4 and L5, with Earth's gravity reversing its direction before each close approach. Several known asteroids (2010 SO16, 419624) occupy such orbits. A small artificial probe in a horseshoe orbit would be essentially indistinguishable from a near-Earth object.

## The Kardashev and Barrow Scales

Before moving to exoplanetary technosignatures, the review introduces two classification frameworks that structure the rest of the paper.

The **Kardashev scale** is well-known: Type I civilizations harness planetary energy ($$\sim 10^{16}$$ watts), Type II stellar energy ($$\sim 10^{26}$$ watts), Type III galactic energy ($$\sim 10^{36}$$ watts). What is less well-known is how severely the Drake equation — SETI's foundational framework — is biased toward Type I civilizations on static planets. The Drake equation assumes civilizations that evolve on a planet, develop radio technology, and broadcast. It does not account for non-communicative technosignatures, dynamic colonization, or the artifacts that would accumulate over millions of years of expansion.

The **Barrow scale** inverts the direction entirely: instead of outward expansion, it measures a civilization's ability to manipulate progressively _smaller_ scales — from planetary engineering down to atmospheric, surface, microscopic, molecular, atomic, and subatomic manipulation. A sufficiently advanced Barrow-scale civilization might be invisible at Kardashev scales. Its technosignatures would not be megastructures or waste heat but anomalous isotope ratios, engineered molecules, or modified physical constants.

The juxtaposition of these two scales is one of the review's quiet insights. Most SETI research implicitly assumes Kardashev-type civilizations — big, energetic, visible. The Barrow scale suggests an entire class of advanced civilizations whose technological signatures would be small, subtle, and embedded in the fabric of matter itself.

## The Cosmic Haystack: Communication from First Principles

The review's treatment of interstellar communication is its most technically dense section, and also its most philosophically interesting. The question is not whether anyone is broadcasting, but: if they were, how would we recognize it?

The fundamental constraints are sobering. The **inverse square law** means signal strength falls as distance squared — a transmitter powerful enough to be detected across the galaxy would require energy budgets exceeding most civilizations' total output. The **speed of light** imposes round-trip delays measured in years or millennia, ruling out conversational exchange. The **Heisenberg uncertainty principle** sets fundamental limits on simultaneous time and frequency precision of any signal.

Within these constraints, the authors analyze **Schelling points** — frequencies at which two parties with no prior communication might converge by reasoning from shared knowledge. Three types are proposed:

1. **Mathematical constants**: frequencies derived from $$\pi$$, $$e$$, or prime numbers — universal truths any technological civilization would know.
2. **Physical constants**: frequencies derived from fundamental physics (fine structure constant, Planck units) — shared by any civilization in our universe.
3. **Astronomical Schelling points**: prominent natural frequencies like the hydrogen 21-cm line, water hole, or spectral features of common molecules.

The review then expands beyond electromagnetic signals entirely. Information could be transmitted via **stellar occulters** (modulating a star's light curve), **neutrinos** (nearly impossible to intercept, but equally difficult to detect), **gravitational waves** (now observable post-LIGO), or **inscribed matter** — physical objects containing encoded information, the interstellar equivalent of a message in a bottle.

The communication topology is itself a design variable. One-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-one, many-to-many — or no communication intent at all, where the technosignature is an unintended byproduct of technological activity. The total dimensionality of this search space — frequency, time, direction, polarization, modulation, carrier type, intent — is what the authors call the **cosmic haystack**. We have explored an almost negligibly small fraction of it.

## Propulsion as Signature

If interstellar travel is possible, it leaves traces. The review catalogs propulsion technologies and their observable signatures:

- **Light sails** would appear as objects with anomalous non-gravitational acceleration (recalling the debate around 'Oumuamua).
- **Ramjets** harvesting interstellar hydrogen would produce characteristic emission signatures.
- **Stellar engines** — mechanisms that move entire stars — would manifest as anomalous stellar velocities or trajectories.
- **Spacetime manipulation** (Alcubierre-type warp drives, traversable wormholes) would produce gravitational wave signatures distinct from natural sources. The review catalogs five distinct wormhole metrics: Ellis-Bronnikov, Morris-Thorne, Visser thin-shell, Teo rotating, and Klinkhamer spacetime-defect solutions.

The key insight is that each propulsion mechanism predicts specific, potentially detectable signatures. A civilization that has mastered interstellar travel has not necessarily mastered stealth.

## The View from Outside

At galactic and extragalactic scales, the review reaches its most speculative territory — and, arguably, its most interesting.

Galaxy-scale technosignatures would include anomalous energy distributions, modified stellar populations, or coordinated activity across multiple star systems. Extragalactic signatures might appear as anomalies in galaxy surveys — systems whose properties deviate from astrophysical expectations in ways consistent with large-scale engineering.

Then there is the **simulation hypothesis**, which the authors treat not as philosophy but as a technosignature prediction framework. If our universe is a simulation, what would we expect to observe?

- **Discrete spacetime**: the GZK cutoff on ultra-high-energy cosmic rays might reflect a computational grid spacing.
- **Anomalous constants**: unexplained fine-tuning of physical constants could be parameter choices by the simulator.
- **Quantum indeterminacy**: wave function collapse might be **lazy evaluation** — the simulation computing definite states only when observed, as a computational efficiency optimization.
- **Computational complexity limits**: the observable universe's finite size and speed-of-light horizon might reflect processing capacity constraints.

The authors are careful to note that none of these would constitute proof of simulation — each has conventional physical explanations. But the framework is productive because it generates specific, testable predictions. That is more than most metaphysical hypotheses offer.

## What Counts as Evidence?

The review's final discussion section addresses the epistemological challenges that make technosignature research uniquely difficult.

The **N=1 problem** is fundamental: we have exactly one example of a technological civilization (ourselves), making it impossible to derive statistical priors. The famous **Wow! Signal** of 1977 illustrates the difficulty — a 72-second narrowband radio signal that matched every criterion for an artificial extraterrestrial transmission, never repeated, never explained, and never confirmed. A single detection, no matter how striking, cannot be distinguished from an instrumental artifact or unknown natural phenomenon without reproducibility.

The authors are refreshingly honest about the boundary between **science fiction and scientific hypothesis**. Many technosignature proposals originate in fiction — Dyson spheres, warp drives, self-replicating probes. The review's implicit argument is that the origin of an idea is irrelevant; what matters is whether it generates testable predictions. A Dyson sphere is a legitimate scientific hypothesis not because Freeman Dyson was a physicist but because it predicts specific infrared excess signatures that can be searched for in astronomical surveys.

## The Structure of the Search

Reading this review, what emerges most clearly is not any single technosignature but the **structure of the problem itself**. The search for extraterrestrial intelligence is not one search but an enormous family of searches, varying along at least a dozen independent dimensions: spatial scale, temporal scale, carrier type, energy scale, intent, technology type, and more.

The Drake equation — SETI's founding framework from 1961 — collapses all of this into a single scalar: the number of communicative civilizations. Vidal et al.'s review makes visible everything that scalar hides. A civilization that has colonized its asteroid belt but never developed radio. A probe in a horseshoe orbit that has been quietly monitoring Earth for a billion years. A message encoded in the non-coding regions of terrestrial DNA. An entire technological civilization operating at the Barrow scale, manipulating atoms rather than stars, invisible to every telescope ever built.

The paper's most important contribution may be this: it does not argue that any of these things exist. It argues that we have barely begun to look.
