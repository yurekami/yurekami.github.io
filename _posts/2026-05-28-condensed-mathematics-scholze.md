Every building needs a foundation, and the foundation for the Clausen–Scholze revolution in mathematics is this document: _Lectures on Condensed Mathematics_,[^notes] originally taught at Bonn in summer 2019, now posted to the arXiv as a "stable citable version" in May 2026.

[^notes]: Peter Scholze (joint with Dustin Clausen), _Lectures on Condensed Mathematics_, arXiv:2605.03658 (May 2026). 78 pages. Originally taught as a course at the University of Bonn, Summer 2019.

If you've been following the posts on [analytic geometry](/blog/2026/analytic-geometry-scholze-clausen/), [complex geometry rebuilt from scratch](/blog/2026/condensed-mathematics-complex-geometry/), [six-functor formalisms](/blog/2026/six-functor-formalisms-scholze/), or the [proof of geometric Langlands](/blog/2026/geometric-langlands-scholze/), you've seen the downstream applications. This is the source — the 78 pages where the key definitions are made, the key theorems proved, and the program launched.

## The Question

> **Question 1.1.** How to do algebra when rings/modules/groups carry a topology?

This question — so basic it seems barely mathematical — is the starting point. The answer, in a sentence: replace topological spaces with **condensed sets**, and everything works.

"Everything works" means: the category of condensed abelian groups is abelian (topological abelian groups are not); it satisfies Grothendieck's axioms (AB3)–(AB6) and (AB3\*)–(AB4\*) — the best possible behavior; it has compact projective generators; it has a well-behaved derived category; and it embeds all compactly generated topological spaces fully faithfully. The fix is not a hack. It is a _better foundation_.

## Condensed Sets

> **Definition 1.2.** A _condensed set_ is a sheaf of sets on the pro-étale site of a point — the category of profinite sets with finite jointly surjective families as covers.

Concretely: a condensed set is a functor $$T: \{\text{profinite sets}\}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathrm{Sets}$$ satisfying a sheaf condition. Every topological space $$X$$ gives a condensed set via $$\underline{X}(S) = \mathrm{Cont}(S, X)$$ — the set of continuous maps from $$S$$ to $$X$$. This functor is faithful on all topological spaces, and fully faithful on compactly generated ones (Proposition 1.7).

The key example that shows condensed sets are _strictly more_ than topological spaces:

> **Example 1.9.** Let $$\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{disc}}$$ be the reals with the discrete topology, and $$\underline{\mathbb{R}}$$ the reals with their natural topology. The map $$\underline{\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{disc}}} \to \underline{\mathbb{R}}$$ is injective, and its cokernel $$Q$$ is a condensed abelian group with "underlying" abelian group $$Q(*) = 0$$. But for a general profinite set $$S$$,
>
> $$Q(S) = \{\text{continuous maps } S \to \mathbb{R}\} / \{\text{locally constant maps } S \to \mathbb{R}\} \neq 0.$$

This is the phantom that topological abelian groups couldn't see: the cokernel of a map that "should" be an isomorphism at the level of points, but isn't at the level of profinite probes. Condensed sets have room for this cokernel. That room is what makes the category abelian.

## Condensed Abelian Groups: The Perfect Category

> **Theorem 1.10 / 2.2.** The category of condensed abelian groups satisfies Grothendieck's axioms (AB3), (AB4), (AB5), (AB6), (AB3\*), (AB4\*). Moreover, it is generated by compact projective objects.

This is the theorem that unlocks everything. Axioms (AB4\*) (exactness of arbitrary products) and (AB6) (a filtered-colimit-product interchange) fail for sheaves on almost every site in algebraic geometry. They hold here because condensed abelian groups have **compact projective generators** — the free condensed abelian groups $$\mathbb{Z}[S]$$ on extremally disconnected sets $$S$$.

An extremally disconnected set is a profinite set in which every surjection splits (equivalently, the Stone–Čech compactification of a discrete set). The key fact (Proposition 2.2): sheaves on the pro-étale site of a point, when evaluated on extremally disconnected sets, are already sheaves — no sheafification needed. This makes $$\mathbb{Z}[S]$$ for extremally disconnected $$S$$ a compact projective object, and the collection of all such generates the entire category.

## Cohomology: Where the Real Numbers Are Not Solid

Lecture III proves the foundational cohomology vanishing:

> **Theorem 3.2.** For a profinite set $$S$$ and $$i > 0$$, $$H^i(S, \mathbb{Z}) = 0$$ and $$H^i(S, \mathbb{R}) = 0$$.

The proof for $$\mathbb{Z}$$ uses Nöbeling's theorem: $$C(S, \mathbb{Z})$$ is free abelian for any profinite $$S$$. The proof for $$\mathbb{R}$$ uses the fact that $$\mathbb{R}$$ is the quotient field of a normed ring, plus an explicit resolution.

The vanishing for $$\mathbb{Z}$$ is what makes solid modules work. The vanishing for $$\mathbb{R}$$ is more subtle — it requires the archimedean metric. This asymmetry foreshadows the split between the solid (non-archimedean) and liquid (archimedean) theories developed in the [analytic geometry lectures](/blog/2026/analytic-geometry-scholze-clausen/).

## Locally Compact Abelian Groups: Pontryagin Duality

Lecture IV proves that condensed mathematics recovers Pontryagin duality for locally compact abelian groups — one of the crown jewels of classical harmonic analysis.

> **Theorem 4.3.** The functor from locally compact abelian groups to condensed abelian groups is exact and fully faithful. Pontryagin duality $$A \mapsto A^D = \mathrm{Hom}(A, \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})$$ extends to the derived category: for locally compact abelian $$A$$, $$R\mathrm{Hom}(\underline{A}, \underline{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}) \cong \underline{A^D}$$ concentrated in degree 0.

This is a nontrivial test: the condensed framework must reproduce classical results, not just abstract ones. The proof goes through an explicit resolution of locally compact abelian groups by products of copies of $$\mathbb{R}$$ and $$\mathbb{Z}$$ (Appendix to Lecture IV).

## Solid Abelian Groups: Completing Everything

Lectures V–VI introduce the first major new algebraic structure: **solid abelian groups**. The motivation is that tensor products of condensed abelian groups are "usually pathological" — for instance, $$\mathbb{Z}_p \otimes \mathbb{R}$$ in condensed abelian groups is not what anyone wants.

> **Definition 5.1.** For a profinite set $$S = \varprojlim S_i$$, define $$\mathbb{Z}[S]^{\blacksquare} := \varprojlim \mathbb{Z}[S_i]$$. A condensed abelian group $$A$$ is _solid_ if every map $$f: S \to A$$ from a profinite set extends uniquely to $$\mathbb{Z}[S]^{\blacksquare} \to A$$.

By Nöbeling's theorem, $$\mathbb{Z}[S]^{\blacksquare} \cong \prod_I \mathbb{Z}$$ for some set $$I$$ — the free solid module on a profinite set is just a product of copies of $$\mathbb{Z}$$. Solid modules are the condensed abelian groups that "respect the profinite topology" by allowing integration against integer-valued measures.

> **Theorem 5.8.** Solid abelian groups form a full abelian subcategory of condensed abelian groups, stable under all limits, colimits, and extensions, with compact projective generators $$\prod_I \mathbb{Z}$$. The inclusion admits a left adjoint $$M \mapsto M^{\blacksquare}$$ (solidification). Moreover, $$D(\mathrm{Solid}) \hookrightarrow D(\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab}))$$ is fully faithful.

The key computation that shows this is a non-archimedean theory:

> **Corollary 6.1.** $$\mathbb{R}^{L\blacksquare} = 0$$. The derived solidification of $$\mathbb{R}$$ vanishes.

The reals are killed by solidification. This is not a bug — it reflects the fact that solidification is an "adic" completion, and $$\mathbb{R}$$ is not adic. But it means the solid theory only covers the non-archimedean world. The archimedean world requires the liquid modules of the [analytic geometry lectures](/blog/2026/analytic-geometry-scholze-clausen/).

The solid tensor product $$\otimes^{\blacksquare}$$ (Theorem 6.2) detects adic topologies beautifully:

$$\mathbb{Z}_p \otimes^{L\blacksquare} \mathbb{Z}_\ell = 0 \text{ for } p \neq \ell, \quad \mathbb{Z}_p \otimes^{L\blacksquare} \mathbb{Z}_p = \mathbb{Z}_p, \quad \mathbb{Z}[[U]] \otimes^{L\blacksquare} \mathbb{Z}[[T]] = \mathbb{Z}[[U, T]].$$

And for a CW complex $$X$$: $$\mathbb{Z}[\underline{X}]^{L\blacksquare} \cong H_\bullet(X)$$ — the derived solidification of the free condensed abelian group on $$X$$ recovers singular homology.

## Analytic Rings: The Abstraction

Lecture VII abstracts the pattern into the key definition of the entire program:

> **Definition 7.4.** An _analytic ring_ is a condensed ring $$A$$ equipped with a "theory of measures" $$\mathcal{M}$$: a functor assigning to each extremally disconnected $$S$$ an $$A$$-module $$\mathcal{M}[S]$$, such that $$R\mathrm{Hom}_A(\mathcal{M}[S], C) \to R\Gamma(S, C)$$ is an isomorphism for all $$S$$ and all complexes $$C$$ built from $$\mathcal{M}[T]$$'s.

The condition says: the abstract notion of "measure" and the concrete notion of "section" agree at the derived level. Different choices of $$\mathcal{M}$$ give different geometries:

- $$\mathbb{Z}^{\blacksquare}$$: the solid theory (Lecture V)
- $$(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{M}_{<p})$$ for $$0 < p \leq 1$$: the liquid theory ([analytic geometry](/blog/2026/analytic-geometry-scholze-clausen/))
- $$(A, \mathbb{Z})^{\blacksquare}$$ for discrete $$A$$: discrete adic geometry
- $$A^{\blacksquare}$$ for finitely generated $$\mathbb{Z}$$-algebras $$A$$: the coherent sheaf theory (Theorem 8.1)

> **Proposition 7.5.** For any analytic ring $$(A, \mathcal{M})$$, the category of $$\mathcal{M}$$-complete modules is abelian, stable under limits/colimits/extensions, with compact projective generators $$\mathcal{M}[S]$$, a symmetric monoidal tensor product, and a fully faithful derived embedding.

This is the template that every subsequent application follows.

## Solid Modules and Coherent Duality

Lecture VIII proves the theorem that makes coherent algebraic geometry work in the condensed framework:

> **Theorem 8.1.** For any finitely generated $$\mathbb{Z}$$-algebra $$A$$, $$A^{\blacksquare}$$ is an analytic ring. The forgetful functor $$D(A^{\blacksquare}) \hookrightarrow D((A, \mathbb{Z})^{\blacksquare})$$ admits a left adjoint $$j^*$$ and a further left adjoint $$j_!$$, satisfying the projection formula.

The key construction: for $$A = \mathbb{Z}[T]$$, the ring $$A_\infty = \mathbb{Z}((T^{-1}))$$ — Laurent series in $$T^{-1}$$ — plays the role of "functions near the boundary of $$\mathrm{Spec}\,A$$." The left adjoint $$j_!$$ is given by $$- \otimes_A (A_\infty/A)[-1]$$. Scholze remarks: "The arguments are roughly similar to the arguments for $$\mathbb{Z}^{\blacksquare}$$, with $$\mathbb{Z}((T^{-1}))$$ taking the role of $$\mathbb{R}$$."

The payoff is **Grothendieck duality** (Theorem 11.1), proved for smooth separated morphisms by a diagonal argument:

> **Theorem 11.1/11.6.** For $$f: X \to \mathrm{Spec}\,R$$ smooth of relative dimension $$d$$, there is a canonical $$f_!: D(\mathcal{O}_{X,\blacksquare}) \to D(R^{\blacksquare})$$ preserving compact objects, with $$f^! R \cong \det(\Omega^1_{X/R})[d]$$ and
>
> $$R\mathcal{H}\mathrm{om}_{\mathcal{O}_X}(C, \omega_{X/R})[d] \cong R\mathrm{Hom}_R(f_! C, R)$$
>
> for all $$C \in D(\mathcal{O}_{X,\blacksquare})$$.

Scholze's concluding remark captures the achievement: "Modulo establishing the formalism and checking various abstract results, this gives a proof of the finiteness of coherent cohomology and Grothendieck duality where the **only computation one has to do is for $$\mathbb{A}^1$$**."

The enabling fact is that solid $$A$$-modules allow $$\prod_I A \otimes_{A^{\blacksquare}} A[1/f]^{\blacksquare} \cong \prod_I A[1/f]$$ — products commute with localization. This fails for abstract $$A$$-modules. The fix makes $$j_!$$ (left adjoint to restriction) exist, and from that single formal fact, the entire theory of coherent duality follows.

Scholze flags what is truly new: "$$f^!$$ preserves discrete objects... however, our definition makes critical use of condensed mathematics! Indeed, $$f_!$$ does _not_ preserve discrete objects, and does not exist (naively) in classical approaches."

## Globalization: Gluing on Adic Spaces

Lectures IX–X prove that the solid module theory globalizes to adic spaces:

> **Theorem 9.8.** The association $$\mathrm{Spa}(A, A^+) \mapsto D((A, A^+)^{\blacksquare})$$ defines a sheaf of $$\infty$$-categories on discrete adic spaces.

The proof reduces to the key computation $$\mathbb{Z}[[T]] \otimes^{L}_{(\mathbb{Z}[T], \mathbb{Z})^{\blacksquare}} \mathbb{Z}((T^{-1})) = 0$$ — the "functions near the boundary" vanish after completing at the boundary. Scholze gives an explicit example (an $$\mathbb{F}_p[T]$$-module that localizes to zero but isn't zero) showing that gluing fails at the abelian level — the $$\infty$$-categorical language, with its homotopy-coherent descent, is _necessary_.

## What This Document Is

These 78 pages are the source code for a mathematical revolution. Every subsequent development — liquid modules, analytic spaces, the Liquid Tensor Experiment, condensed complex geometry, the six-functor formalism on analytic stacks — traces back to the definitions and theorems established here.

The core insight is deceptively simple: replace topological spaces by sheaves on profinite sets. But the consequences are vast. Topological abelian groups become an abelian category. Completed tensor products become a derived functor. Pontryagin duality extends to the derived category. Coherent duality and finiteness of cohomology reduce to a single computation on $$\mathbb{A}^1$$. And the non-archimedean and archimedean worlds are revealed as two specializations of a single algebraic framework — the theory of analytic rings.

That the arXiv version appears seven years after the original lectures, as a "stable citable version with mostly cosmetic changes," is itself a statement: the foundations laid in 2019 have held up. The building stands.
