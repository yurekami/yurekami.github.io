Shattering Triples with Six Permutations: A Machine-Found Bound and Its Lean Formalization

Created on July 06, 2026

2026   ·   mathematics   combinatorics   extremal-combinatorics   lean   formalization   ai-for-math   ·   research

Recent work by Binghui Peng, Runzhou Tao, Steven Wang, Hantao Yu, and Diyi Liu1 showed that a simple prover–verifier loop — one model proposes proofs, another adversarially checks them, humans referee, and a Lean pipeline machine-checks the algebra — can resolve genuinely open problems, provided you point it at the right targets. Their nine solutions were drawn not from famous conjectures but from curated, precisely-stated, neglected problems: the COLT open-problem track, a FOCS paper, and Glaz’s list of open problems in commutative ring theory.

I wanted to see the envelope for myself. So I rebuilt the harness — scout a curated open-problem list, extract precisely-stated targets, and attack each with up to four rounds of a max-effort prover against a panel of adversarial verifiers, then a final gauntlet of independent skeptics — and ran it. This post is about the one clean result it produced, the honest calibration of what it didn’t do, and the part I care about most: what it takes to get from “a language model emitted a proof” to “the Lean kernel accepts it, and here is exactly which line still rests on a computer.”

The headline, stated carefully:

Claim (candidate). For the Johnson–Wickes shattering limit \(L\) of BCC30 Problem 30.14, the recorded bounds \(\tfrac{17}{42} \le L \le \tfrac{47}{60}\) can be improved to

\[\frac{17}{42} \;\le\; L \;\le\; \frac{19}{28} \approx 0.6786,\]

via the exact values \(f(6)=16,\; f(7)=26,\; f(8)=40,\; f(9)=57\).

I call it a candidate deliberately: the upper bounds come from an exhaustive computer search that I have not formalized, and no human combinatorialist has yet refereed it. What is fully machine-checked — down to the Lean kernel with the axioms enumerated — is the reduction from those finite facts to the bound, and the monotonicity lemma that reduction rests on. More on that boundary below.

The problem

Fix \([n] = \{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}\). A family \(\mathcal{P}\) of permutations of \([n]\) shatters a \(k\)-set \(X \subseteq [n]\) if the elements of \(X\) appear in all \(k!\) possible relative orders across the permutations of \(\mathcal{P}\). Since a triple has \(3! = 6\) orders, six permutations is the minimum that could possibly shatter one — and a triple is shattered by a family of six iff the six induced orders are pairwise distinct.

Johnson and Wickes2 ask: how many triples of \([n]\) can a family of six permutations shatter at once? Write \(f(n)\) for the maximum. An averaging argument (which we will meet again shortly) shows that \(f(n)/\binom{n}{3}\) is non-increasing in \(n\), so it converges to a limit

\[L \;=\; \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{\binom{n}{3}}.\]

The published record is \(\tfrac{17}{42} \le L \le \tfrac{47}{60}\), with \(f(5) = 8\) known exactly. The problem is a textbook target for this kind of pipeline: precisely stated, essentially unattacked since 2024 (two papers cite the BCC30 collection; neither touches 30.14), and — crucially — settleable at the finite level by a search a program can re-run.

The pipeline, and what it did not do

The selection philosophy is the whole game. Famous conjectures are famous because a great many capable people have already tried the obvious things; a language model has no edge there. Neglected problems on curated lists are different: often nobody has run the obvious careful computation or written the obvious few-page argument, and that is exactly the long tail where a tireless generator with a ruthless verifier has an advantage. My scout swept eight open-problem sources — among them the Erdős problem database, the COLT track, sublinear.info, the Open Problem Garden, recent arXiv “open problems” sections, and the remaining Glaz problems — and a three-judge panel ranked the candidates by openness, tractability, verifiability, precision, and neglectedness.

I want to be candid about the base rate. Of six targets I attacked seriously, one produced a clean, verifiable result — the one below. The others are instructive failures:

  • Melnikov’s valency-variety conjecture (a 1960s graph-colouring problem): the loop proved it for triangle-free graphs, for \(K_4\)-free graphs with \(\chi \neq 3\), and for the extremal antiregular case, and reduced the whole conjecture to a single window of \(\chi = 3\) graphs — then stalled precisely where a stability argument was needed.
  • Erdős #617 (balanced \(r\)-colourings of \(K_{r^2+1}\), case \(r=5\)): it produced a genuinely new lemma — the affine-plane balanced colouring of \(K_{r^2}\) never extends by one vertex, for any \(r\) — and reduced the case to a finite Ramsey-type residual, then could not close it.
  • Canonne’s COLT 2024 open question on instance-optimal identity testing: it gave a complete conceptual fix to the gap Canonne flags in a Valiant–Valiant lower bound, modulo one analytic lemma it could verify numerically but not prove.

The pattern is consistent and, I think, the honest summary of where this technology sits: the loop reliably drives a neglected problem down to a single sharp remaining lemma, and then stalls exactly where genuine new theory is required. It is a sweeper of the long tail, not a substitute for the hard creative step. The one problem it swept clean is the rest of this post.

The result

The quantity \(f(5) = 8\) is published. The pipeline computed the next four values exactly:

\[f(6) = 16, \qquad f(7) = 26, \qquad f(8) = 40, \qquad f(9) = 57.\]

To turn these into a bound on \(L\) we need the monotonicity of the ratio, which is worth stating precisely because everything hinges on it.

Lemma 1 (restriction / monotonicity). For every \(n \ge 4\),

\[(n-3)\, f(n) \;\le\; n\, f(n-1).\]

Equivalently, writing \(F(n) = f(n)/\binom{n}{3}\), the sequence \(F\) is non-increasing.

The proof is an averaging argument. Take an extremal family \(\mathcal{F}\) on \([n]\) with \(\operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F}) = f(n)\). For each value \(x \in [n]\), delete \(x\) from every permutation to obtain a family \(\mathcal{F}\vert_x\) on the remaining \(n-1\) points. Two facts:

  1. Deleting a value outside a triple \(T\) leaves the relative order of \(T\)’s elements unchanged, so for \(x \notin T\), the triple \(T\) is shattered by \(\mathcal{F}\) iff it is shattered by \(\mathcal{F}\vert_x\). Summing over all \(x\) and counting each shattered triple once per value it avoids gives the identity

    \[\sum_{x \in [n]} \operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F}\vert_x) \;=\; (n-3)\, \operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F}),\]

    because each 3-element triple avoids exactly \(n - 3\) of the \(n\) values.

  2. Each \(\mathcal{F}\vert_x\) is a family of six permutations on an \((n-1)\)-element set, so \(\operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F}\vert_x) \le f(n-1)\).

Combining, \((n-3) f(n) = \sum_x \operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F}\vert_x) \le n\, f(n-1)\). Dividing by \(\binom{n}{3}\) and using \(\binom{n}{3} / \binom{n-1}{3} = n/(n-3)\) turns this into \(F(n) \le F(n-1)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Because \(F\) is non-increasing, \(L \le F(m)\) for every \(m\). The values above give a descending ladder:

\[F(5) = \tfrac{8}{10} = 0.8, \quad F(7) = \tfrac{26}{35} \approx 0.7429, \quad F(8) = \tfrac{40}{56} \approx 0.7143, \quad F(9) = \tfrac{57}{84} = \tfrac{19}{28} \approx 0.6786.\]

Notice that the improvement over the recorded \(47/60 \approx 0.7833\) already appears at \(f(7) = 26\), which alone gives \(L \le 26/35\); the value \(f(6) = 16\) gives only \(L \le 4/5\) and does not beat the record. Pushing the exhaustive search out to \(f(9) = 57\) yields the stated \(L \le 19/28\).

The engine underneath is a symmetry-reduced restriction/extension tower: relabelling and reversal reduce the search to families containing the identity, and Lemma 1’s identity, run in reverse, lets a complete list of near-extremal families on \([n-1]\) be extended by one point to certify \(f(n)\) on \([n]\). That is how the computation reaches \(n = 9\) without enumerating all of \(\binom{\lvert S_9 \rvert}{6}\) families.

Verification

A language model emitting a plausible-looking proof is worth nothing on its own; the value is entirely in the verification. This result went through several independent gates before I was willing to write it down.

  • An adversarial verifier panel during generation: each prover round was checked by three independent verifiers — a line-by-line logic referee, a computational counterexample-hunter, and a statement-fidelity checker — and only claims that no verifier could break survived.
  • A clean-room reproduction of the entire tower by an agent with no access to the original code, which independently reproduced every value \(f(5..8)\), the ceiling \(f(9) \le 57\), and every orbit-class count in the enumeration.
  • A direct, extension-lemma-free confirmation of \(f(7) = 26\). I wrote my own from-scratch branch-and-bound and ran the exhaustive search to completion — 9,554,802 nodes over about 5.6 hours — verifying that no family of six permutations of \([7]\) shatters 27 triples. Combined with an explicit family attaining 26, this pins \(f(7) = 26\) by a route that shares nothing with the tower. Since \(f(7) = 26\) already beats \(47/60\), the improvement rests on two fully independent computations.
  • My own recount of every witness family with a shatter-counter written directly from the definition, which also reproduces the published \(f(5) = 8\) example as a sanity check.

None of this is a proof in the sense a journal would accept — the completeness of the exhaustive enumeration is a computer-assisted claim — but it is the discipline that separates a candidate worth formalizing from a hallucination.

Formalizing it in Lean 4

This is the part I find most clarifying. If you actually build the Lean development, you are forced to say precisely which facts are theorems and which are imported from a computer you are asking the reader to trust. The trust boundary stops being rhetorical and becomes an enumeration of axioms.

The development is three files.

Certificate.lean — self-contained core Lean 4, no Mathlib. It encodes permutations as lists, defines the shatter count directly from the definition, and proves, by decide (kernel reduction only):

\[\texttt{shCount}\; \texttt{fam7} = 26, \qquad \texttt{shCount}\; \texttt{fam8} = 40, \qquad \texttt{shCount}\; \texttt{fam9} = 57,\]

for explicit families, together with the fact that each family really is six permutations. Running #print axioms fam9_sh reports “does not depend on any axioms.” These lower bounds \(f(7) \ge 26,\; f(8) \ge 40,\; f(9) \ge 57\) are therefore verified trusting nothing beyond the Lean kernel — no native_decide, no Mathlib, no sorry.

ShatterBound.lean — the Mathlib layer. It defines the extremal function \(f(n)\) as a genuine maximum over all families \(\mathrm{Fin}\,6 \to \mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Fin}\,n)\), the ratio \(F\), the limit \(L = \bigsqcap_n F(n)\), and proves the arithmetic reduction: from \(f(9) = 57\), the ratio \(F(9) = 57/84 = 19/28\), hence \(L \le 19/28\), and \(19/28 < 47/60\). The key rational-arithmetic step is a Nat.choose identity, \((k+1)\binom{k}{3} = (k-2)\binom{k+1}{3}\), proved from first principles.

Lemma1.lean — the piece I most wanted to get right. It proves Lemma 1 itself, from scratch. The four moving parts:

  1. cmp3_congr: the induced-order code of a triple depends only on the three pairwise “\(<\)” relations among positions, so any order-preserving relabeling leaves it unchanged.
  2. restrict F x: deleting a value \(x\) from every permutation to get a family on \(\mathrm{Fin}\,(n-1)\), built with Fin.succAbove and Function.invFun. Order-preservation falls straight out of Fin.succAbove_lt_succAbove_iff.
  3. shCount_restrict_eq: a Finset.card_bij bijection showing that the triples of \([n]\) avoiding \(x\) that \(\mathcal{F}\) shatters correspond exactly to the triples of \([n-1]\) shattered by the restricted family — hence \(\operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F}\vert_x) \le f(n-1)\).
  4. sum_shCountAvoiding: the double-count identity \(\sum_x \operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F}\vert_x) = (n-3)\operatorname{sh}(\mathcal{F})\).

Assembling these gives restriction_bound : (n-3) * f n ≤ n * f (n-1) for \(n \ge 4\). And #print axioms restriction_bound reports only propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound — the three standard Mathlib axioms. No sorry, no native_decide. Lemma 1 is genuinely kernel-checked mathematics.

Here is the trust boundary, stated as an enumeration rather than a promise. After discharging Lemma 1, exactly one input remains unformalized: the exhaustive-search upper bounds \(f(6) \le 16,\; f(7) \le 26,\; f(8) \le 40,\; f(9) \le 57\). These enter the Lean theorems only as an explicit hypothesis \(f(9) \le 57\), so every theorem is unconditionally true as stated (“if \(f(9) \le 57\), then \(L \le 19/28\)”). Nothing launders the computer search into an unconditional claim. Everything else — the definitions, the lower-bound witnesses (with zero axioms), Lemma 1, and the whole reduction — is proved. Closing the last gap would mean formalizing a verified exhaustive search in Lean, which is a genuinely separate project.

I find this a much more honest end state than “an AI proved a theorem.” What an AI did was: propose the values, propose the arguments, and — with a great deal of adversarial checking and one long overnight search — get to a point where a proof assistant will tell you, axiom by axiom, exactly how much of the claim is unconditional and exactly which finite computation you are still being asked to trust.

What this is, and is not

It is not a breakthrough. It is a bounded improvement to one inequality on a niche extremal-combinatorics problem — the same envelope the pipeline-math authors demonstrated — computer-assisted, and in need of a human referee before it should be cited as a theorem. The exact value of \(L\) remains open, and the lower bound \(17/42\) is untouched.

What I think it is: a small piece of evidence about where the frontier actually sits. A prover–verifier loop, aimed at precisely-stated neglected problems and married to a verification discipline strict enough to survive Lean, can produce genuine, referee-able increments and can drive harder problems down to a single sharp lemma. It stalls exactly at the step that has always been hard. That step — the new idea — is still ours. The long tail of careful computations and few-page arguments that nobody got around to is, increasingly, not.

The Lean development (three files, ~525 lines, lake build clean, no sorry, with Lemma 1 kernel-checked) and my from-scratch search verifiers are on GitHub: yurekami/shatter-bound. Clone it and run #print axioms restriction_bound yourself, or re-run the \(f(7)\) search.
