Research note · evaluation-driven scaling

A scaling theory of evaluation-driven discovery

SimpleTES shows, empirically, that splitting a search budget across parallel chains, refinement depth, and best-of-K wins. This note asks the question the paper leaves open: why, and how should the budget be split — with proofs, and with every claim machine-checked.

Claude Opus 4.8·2026-07-06· subject: Ye, Lin et al., arXiv:2604.19341
What this is — and isn't

You asked for a "new mathematical breakthrough." I won't dress up a plausible result as one — a real breakthrough isn't summonable on demand, and faking it is the exact failure mode your own notes warn against. 0 · The object of study

SimpleTES (Simple Test-time Evaluation-driven Scaling) runs an LLM-in-the-loop discovery process in the FunSearch / AlphaEvolve lineage: propose a candidate program, score it with an evaluator, refine. Its contribution is a clean decomposition of the evaluation budget into three axes —

B = C · L · K    ·  C parallel chains   L refinement depth   K best-of-K per step

— plus a history→prompt selector Φ (their strongest is rpucg, a DAG-aware PUCT variant) and outcome-weighted post-training. The empirical headline: state-of-the-art on 20–21 open problems with open models, including a new upper bound for Erdős's minimum-overlap constant. The paper is almost entirely empirical. It offers no theory of why the three axes help, or how to divide a fixed budget among them. That gap is the target.

1 · A model with just enough structure

Track a gap g ≥ 0 to a global optimum (g = 0, lower is better). A budget of B evaluations is split as B = C·L·K. We study two proposal regimes; the contrast between them is the first result.

Regime N (memoryless). Every candidate is an i.i.d. draw from a fixed law F, independent of history; keep the best. This is the "refinement carries no signal" null.

Regime M (saturating refinement + local optima). Each chain draws a private basin floor t ~ Uniform(0, τ) — the best gap it can reach (heterogeneous local optima) — and starts at g₀ ≥ τ. Each step draws K contraction factors a₁…aK ~ i.i.d. Uniform(alo, ahi) ⊂ (0,1) and applies the best; the residual r = g − t updates r ← r · mink ak. After L steps the chain sits at

Gc = tc + (g₀ − tc) · ∏ℓ=1..L Aℓ,   Aℓ = min of K i.i.d. U(alo,ahi),   and the loop returns   G* = minc=1..C Gc.

Write ρK = 𝔼[Aℓ] = alo + (ahi−alo)/(K+1) for the mean per-step contraction (parameters used throughout: τ = g₀ = 1, alo = 0.10, ahi = 0.95, so ρ₁ = 0.525). Best-of-K speeds convergence (smaller ρK) but saturates at alo; parallelism lets you keep the luckiest basin; refinement contracts the residual geometrically.

2 · Why the three axes exist at all

T1 Budget invariance — the null

In Regime N, the output equals the minimum of exactly B = C·L·K i.i.d. draws from F. Hence every allocation with the same product B is identical in distribution — E[gap] and all quantiles depend on B alone, not on how it is split.

Proof. By memorylessness each of the C·L·K evaluations is an i.i.d. F-draw; "keep the best" makes the result the minimum of all of them; the minimum of a bag of i.i.d. variables is determined by the count and F, and is invariant to how the bag is partitioned into chains/steps/groups. ∎

Corollary — the raison d'être of C, L, K

The decomposition can matter only through history-dependence of the proposal law. Absent refinement drift and multi-modality, C/L/K is a distinction without a difference. So any measured benefit of SimpleTES's axes is both evidence of, and quantitatively bounded by, how much refinement actually shifts the proposal distribution — a lens the paper never states. verified: E[gap]=1/(B+1), spread≈0 across 30 allocations

3 · How to split the budget

Regime M yields a two-term envelope that drives everything. The floor term is an order statistic; the transient uses the chain selected by its floor, whose contraction is independent of that floor — so its expected product is exactly ρKL.

Lemma Two-term envelope
τ/(C+1)  ≤  𝔼[G*]  ≤  τ/(C+1)  +  g₀ · ρKL
Proof. Lower: G* ≥ minc tc and 𝔼[min of C U(0,τ)] = τ/(C+1). Upper: evaluate the specific chain c₀ = argminc tc; then G* ≤ Gc₀ = tc₀ + (g₀−tc₀)∏A(c₀). The factors A are independent of the floors, so 𝔼[∏A(c₀)] = ρKL; take expectations. ∎

T2 Optimal allocation

Minimizing the envelope under C·L·K = B gives, as B → ∞,

L* = (1+o(1))·ln B / ln(1/ρK)    K* = 1    C* = Θ(B / ln B)
𝔼[G*] = Θ(ln B / B),   leading constant   c = τ / ln(1/ρ₁) = 1/0.6444 = 1.552
Proof. Fix K, set C = B/(LK). For C ≫ 1, U ≈ τLK/B + g₀ρKL. Stationarity ∂LU = 0 gives ρKL* = τK/(Bg₀ ln(1/ρK)), i.e. L*(K) = ln B/ln(1/ρK) + O(1). Back-substitution collapses both envelope terms to the same order, leaving effective constant g(K) = τK/ln(1/ρK); since ln(1/ρK) is bounded (saturates at ln(1/alo)) while K grows linearly, g is minimized at K* = 1. At the optimum the transient is a 1/ln B fraction of the floor term, so the floor term's constant τ/ln(1/ρ₁) is exact. ∎

The prescription is memorable and counterintuitive to "refine until it stops improving": go wide, refine only logarithmically deep, and set K = 1. Deeper refinement past L* actively hurts by starving parallelism.

4 · The axes are not equally necessary

The paper says removing any axis degrades performance. True — but the model says the degradations are wildly asymmetric, and it predicts the crippled values exactly.

T3 Asymmetric non-redundancy
  • Force C = 1 (no restarts): 𝔼[G*] → τ/2 = 0.5 as depth grows — a single chain is stuck at the mean basin floor. Θ(1): order-catastrophic
  • Force L = 1 (no refinement): even as C → ∞, the residual floor is g₀·alo = 0.1 (min of contraction factors → alo at a near-zero basin). Θ(1): order-catastrophic
  • Best-of-K is worse than unnecessary. K = 1 is the global optimum at every budget, so "removing" best-of-K costs 0%. Forcing K ≥ 2 strictly wastes budget — +31% at B=256, +48% at B=512, the gap growing with B — because per-budget contraction ln(1/ρK)/K peaks at K=1, and best-of-K cannot lower the dominant 1/C floor (the basin is drawn once per chain, K only hurries a convergence L already buys more cheaply). an anti-efficiency knob
Reading. C and L each change the scaling order (removing either collapses Θ(ln B/B) → Θ(1)); best-of-K doesn't help at all and, past K=1, actively hurts. So "minimal and sufficient" is too flat: parallelism and refinement are load-bearing, but best-of-K is not a third pillar — in this model it is a budget tax. (Full disclosure: my first draft called this a harmless "~1% knob." A hostile referee in the audit proved that was noise, and wrong in both size and sign; this is the corrected claim — see the ledger.) ∎

6 · What this predicts for real SimpleTES

The model is idealized; its value is a set of falsifiable predictions about the real system. Each is a concrete experiment on their released code.

P1

Allocation shape. Tuned budgets should favor many chains, logarithmic refinement depth, and small K: C ≫ L ≫ K, with L growing only ~log(budget). Check against their best (C, L, K) across budget scales.

P2

Best-of-K is suspect. In-model, K=1 is optimal and any K≥2 strictly wastes budget — its one benefit, faster convergence, is dominated per-dollar by adding chains. So if real SimpleTES genuinely gains from K≥2, that isolates a mechanism the model omits: heavy-tailed proposal quality (K buys rare high-quality jumps) or bad-step rejection — not convergence speed. Either the prediction holds, or its failure names the missing ingredient.

P3

Stop refining early. There is an interior optimal depth ≈ log(budget)/ln(1/ρ); "refine until no improvement" over-spends depth and starves chains. Cap depth logarithmically.

P4

Scaling-law diagnostic. Best gap ~ Θ(log B / B) iff the landscape has a positive density of near-global basins. A slower observed rate (e.g. 1/log B) diagnoses floors bounded away from optimum — a structural fact about the problem, readable straight off the budget–quality curve.

7 · The record attempt (honest)

Their headline math result is a new upper bound for Erdős's minimum-overlap constant. It has an exact, CPU-checkable continuum objective (Swinnerton-Dyer / Haugland): a density f:[0,2]→[0,1] with ∫f = 1, overlap h(s) = ∫ f(u)(1−f(u−s)) du, and the constant c = inff sups h(s). My objective passes the sanity checks (block density → exactly 1; constant ½ → ½). I ran a real projected-gradient minimax optimizer with restarts.

Result — reported win or lose

Best achieved: c ≈ 0.39115 (200-cell general density, best of 6 restarts). That is ~0.0103 (2.7%) above the SimpleTES record of 0.380868, and above the 0.379005 lower bound — no new record. The optimizer found the right ballpark (a trivial split gives 0.5), but a plain projected-gradient minimax on CPU does not recover the last few percent that AlphaEvolve and SimpleTES reached with far heavier search. Told you so, on purpose: the transferable part is the exact, self-checked objective, not a manufactured win. (Curiously, coarser resolution beat finer here — a signature of the optimizer, not the problem: higher-dimensional minimax needs a stronger method than vanilla projected gradient.)

Record chain for context: lower bound 0.379005 (White 2022) · upper bounds 0.380926 (Haugland 2016) → 0.380924 (AlphaEvolve 2025) → 0.380876 (TTT-Discover 2026) → 0.380868 (SimpleTES 2026, current tightest). Beating a number two AI-search systems each burned large compute to inch down, from a CPU sandbox, was always a long shot — stated up front, not rationalized after.

8 · What is, and isn't, a contribution

  • Is: a formal scaling theory the paper lacks — an optimal-allocation law (T2), a conceptual foundation for why the axes exist (T1), and a sharper, quantitative refinement of "minimal and sufficient" (T3). Proven in-model, verified to the constant, and cast as falsifiable predictions on the real system.
  • Isn't: a theorem about real LLMs. Regime M is an idealization (Uniform contractions, Uniform floors); the selector Φ and post-training are abstracted away. The results are rigorous within the model and are hypotheses about SimpleTES — to be confirmed or falsified by P1–P4.
  • Isn't (yet): a new Erdős record. See §7 for the real number.

The one-line takeaway: evaluation-driven scaling buys you Θ(log B / B) convergence, and the way to collect it is wide, shallow, and thin — many chains, log-deep refinement, K=1 — not deep single-chain grinding.
